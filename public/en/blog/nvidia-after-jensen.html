The founder is gone. The order book is not.

On Thursday, November 12, Nvidia's board announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while keeping her CFO duties, and director Harvey C. Jones — a former Synopsys CEO — becomes the company's first-ever board chair. The board says it will search inside and outside the company for a permanent chief executive, and gave no timeline.

According to the 8-K filing, the board formally accepted the resignation on November 11, the night before the announcement. The release itself gives no reason for the departure, but the backdrop is not a mystery.

How we got here: five days that ended a 33-year run

On Sunday, November 8, the board opened an independent investigation after reports linked Huang to a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed International whom U.S. authorities reportedly suspected of working for China. Huang acknowledged the affair in an email to staff, while the intelligence allegations remained under investigation. Nvidia said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised — and the espionage claim remains unproven. A subsequent report of a second relationship, this one with a senior Nvidia executive, appears to have tipped the balance; as Forbes noted, most analysts had expected Huang to survive before that second report surfaced. A person familiar with the matter told CNBC the board decided to oust him the night before the announcement.

The market's verdict, day one

The stock had already been bleeding. Nvidia's market value slid from roughly $5.78 trillion on October 6 to $4.51 trillion by November 6, according to YCharts data cited by Decrypt — before the investigation was even public. On Monday, November 9, shares fell as much as 8.8% and closed down about 4.3%, erasing roughly $192 billion even as the broader market rallied.

Thursday's resignation produced a violent but telling session. Shares fell as much as 14.3% intraday before recovering much of the loss, and by midday were down about 5% near $165. Market capitalization dipped below $4 trillion for the first time since July 2025, and Nvidia slipped to third place behind Apple and Microsoft. Notably, retail traders rushed in to buy the dip — Vanda Research recorded the largest opening bid from individual investors it has ever tracked.

What actually changed

The business didn't change on Thursday. What changed is who decides where it goes after 2028.

The pain spread through the ecosystem Nvidia funds and supplies: CoreWeave fell nearly 12%, Nebius about 17% and IREN roughly 8%, per CNBC. And because Nvidia represents more than 6% of the S&P 500, this is not just an Nvidia shareholder's problem — every index fund owner is exposed.

On the cautious end, Questar Capital's Richard Reyle went as far as calling the stock "uninvestable" given the lack of a succession plan, and Gabelli's Ryuta Makino told Bloomberg he expects Nvidia to lose market share. On the other side, Janus Henderson's Jeremiah Buckley argued the resignation could actually be good for the shares by removing the cloud of uncertainty.

Short term: headlines over fundamentals

The next one to three months will be driven by the news cycle more than by the order book.

1. November 17 is the first real test

Nvidia reports fiscal Q3 2027 results on Tuesday, November 17. It had guided to $108 billion in quarterly revenue, with analysts expecting roughly 91% growth. Melius' Reitzes sees upside to both the print and the January-quarter outlook — but also expects fundamentals to take a backseat for now. The market will listen less to the numbers than to Kress's tone on backlog, customer commitments and the roadmap. A clean beat with confident guidance would be the strongest signal that the machine runs without its founder.

2. Headline risk is not over

The independent investigation is still running, the espionage questions are unresolved, Huang has reportedly agreed to testify before Congress, and a shareholder lawsuit is in play. Forbes flags a further watch item: how Washington responds on export controls. Each of these can move the stock independent of earnings.

3. Volatility in both directions

With the stock down roughly 26% month-to-date by one analyst's count, a lot of bad news is already in the price. Record retail buying, institutional de-risking and index weight create a tug-of-war that typically means wide daily swings until a permanent CEO is named.

Long term: the next bet, not the current one

The strongest bullish argument is structural. The CUDA software ecosystem, developer base, networking stack and integrated hardware platform do not resign with the CEO. StoneX notes the roadmap is already defined through the Feynman generation in 2028, Nvidia has sized Blackwell and Rubin demand at $1 trillion through 2027, and long-term supply agreements tie customers to the platform regardless of who leads it.

The bearish argument is about what comes after 2028. Huang ran Nvidia with an unusually flat structure and dozens of direct reports; Fortune described a company where decision-making was concentrated in his hands, and he had openly dismissed conventional succession planning. Axios quoted Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld calling it ridiculous that no successor had been identified. Huang's personal relationships with hyperscaler and server-maker CEOs, and his instinct for the next platform shift — from gaming to crypto to AI — are exactly what investors were paying a premium for.

History offers both templates. Apple under Tim Cook became more valuable than it ever was under Steve Jobs, by executing brilliantly on an established platform. Other founder exits produced a lost decade. The deciding variable will be the permanent CEO. Names floated in the press include internal candidates such as CUDA architect Ian Buck, and outsiders such as Arm's Rene Haas, a former Nvidia executive. An internal engineer-leader would likely reassure the market faster than a financial or outside appointment.

Three scenarios to frame the stock

These are frames for the debate, not a probability model.

Bull case

A strong November 17 report and confident guidance show demand untouched. The investigation closes without findings of compromised secrets. Within 6–9 months the board names a credible technical leader. The "Jensen discount" fades, and the stock re-rates toward the pre-scandal multiple as earnings keep compounding.

Base case

Earnings are solid, but the stock trades at a lower multiple than before while Kress runs the company for roughly a year. Fundamentals through 2027–28 hold. The shares grind sideways-to-higher with high volatility. Analyst targets clustered around $185–$225 reflect this middle path.

Bear case

The investigation produces damaging findings, export controls tighten, key engineers or executives leave, and a drawn-out or unconvincing CEO search erodes customer confidence. Competitors — AMD and custom hyperscaler chips — gain share, and the market applies a lasting governance discount.

The bottom line

In the short run, Nvidia's stock will trade on headlines, the November 17 numbers and the credibility of its interim leadership. In the long run, it will trade on whether the company can keep inventing the next platform without the person who invented the last three. The order book through 2028 suggests the near-term business is safe. The multiple investors are willing to pay for what comes after is now genuinely open to debate.
