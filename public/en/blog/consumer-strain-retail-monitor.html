Still Spending, Starting to Strain — What October's Retail Data Means for Stocks

Still carrying the economy, with a heavier step

The American consumer has carried this economy for two years, and October's numbers say the consumer is still carrying it — just with a heavier step. According to the latest CNBC/NRF Retail Monitor, retail sales rose at their weakest monthly pace since November 2025 as high prices squeezed household budgets. Spending did not fall. For investors, the gap between "not falling" and "healthy" is exactly where the next market move will be decided.

What the data showed

Total retail spending reached $561.7 billion on a seasonally adjusted basis in October, up 0.2% from September and 3.6% from a year earlier. Core retail, which strips out restaurants, rose 0.3% to $454.2 billion. NRF said a strong Halloween season and an early start to holiday promotions kept the number from being weaker — in other words, some of October's spending was borrowed from November and December.

The inflation problem hiding inside the headline

The timing of this report is what makes it uncomfortable. One day earlier, the government said consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, more than expected, with core CPI up 0.3%. Put the two side by side and the picture sharpens: nominal retail sales grew about 0.15–0.2% in a month when prices rose about 0.4%. On a rough inflation-adjusted basis, shoppers bought less stuff in October, not more. The dollars are going up because the price tags are going up.

NRF's own chief economist, Mark Mathews, warned in the federation's holiday outlook that inflation, rising gas prices and household pressure would hit income groups unevenly. Corporate earnings are already confirming it.

The K-shape shows up in earnings

At the top of the income ladder, demand is holding up well. Ralph Lauren (RL) grew revenue 9% and raised its fiscal 2027 outlook, Tapestry (TPR) lifted revenue 11% on a 16% jump at Coach, and eBay (EBAY) grew revenue 14%. Lower down, the tone changes: McDonald's (MCD) saw U.S. comparable sales slip 0.2% and leaned on value promotions, while Hershey (HSY) management openly described a consumer growing more cautious.

The consumer is sorting

The consumer is not breaking. The consumer is sorting — between luxury and value, between essentials and everything else.

How this feeds into the stock market

1. The Fed is the main channel

Soft volume plus hot prices is the mix central bankers like least. The Fed already raised rates in September after nine months on hold, and Governor Waller has since opened the door to a possible half-point increase. A retail slowdown that does not cool inflation does not buy the market any relief on rates. It keeps yields elevated, which weighs most on long-duration growth and high-multiple tech.

2. Discretionary splits in two

Brands serving affluent shoppers (RL, TPR) and value or warehouse players that win when households trade down (COST, WMT, TJX) have the cleaner setup. Costco just added a sweetener with a $30-per-share special dividend. Mid-tier discretionary, home furnishings (RH, WSM, W) and quick-service restaurants are most exposed to the squeezed middle.

3. Electronics, and the AI link

A 9.3% annual gain in electronics and appliances shows device upgrades are still happening, which supports names such as BBY and the consumer-hardware side of big tech.

4. A tailwind for defensives

When the consumer bends but does not break, staples, healthcare and quality balance sheets tend to outperform, and the equal-weight index can lag the cap-weighted one less than in pure tech rallies.

Three scenarios into year-end

The probabilities below are editorial judgement, not a forecast model.

Base case · about 50%

Bend, don't break

Holiday spending lands close to NRF's forecast of 4.2% growth to about $1.013 trillion, helped by promotions. Inflation stays sticky, the Fed hikes another quarter-point in December, and stocks trade sideways with sharp rotations.

  • Winners: value retailers, luxury brands, staples.
  • Laggards: home furnishings, mid-tier apparel, fast food.
  • Indexes: choppy. The Dow and the equal-weight S&P hold up better than the Nasdaq.
Bull case · about 20%

Holiday beat and cooler prices

Shoppers deliver a strong Black Friday–Cyber Monday stretch, November CPI cools, and the Fed signals a pause after December. Real spending turns positive again and the strain narrative fades.

  • Winners: broad consumer discretionary, small caps, retail ETFs such as XRT.
  • Indexes: a year-end rally led by cyclicals, with tech joining as yields ease.
Bear case · about 30%

Pull-forward hangover plus a hawkish Fed

October's early promotions prove to have stolen holiday demand, November and December disappoint, and inflation stays hot enough to push the Fed toward a half-point move. Retailers cut guidance in January.

  • Losers: discretionary retail, restaurants, small caps, high-multiple growth.
  • Relative havens: staples, healthcare, short-duration Treasuries.
  • Indexes: renewed pressure, with the Nasdaq most sensitive to higher yields.

The bottom line

October did not show a consumer in retreat. It showed a consumer paying more to get less. That matters for stocks less through retail sales themselves than through what it does to inflation and the Fed. The single most important data point over the next six weeks is not another sales print. It is whether November's CPI cools while holiday spending holds. If both happen, the strain story fades. If spending slows and prices do not, the market will have to price a Fed that keeps tightening into a softening consumer.

For long-term investors, this is an environment for selectivity rather than broad bets: companies with pricing power, loyal high-income customers, or a value proposition that wins share in a trade-down.
