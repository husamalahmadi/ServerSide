What happened on Friday?

Friday, June 5, 2026 delivered one of the sharpest single-session selloffs of the year, led by a violent collapse in semiconductor and AI-related stocks. The catalyst came from Broadcom, which disappointed investors by leaving its full-year AI chip revenue outlook unchanged — a projection the market had hoped would be revised sharply upward. The response was swift and merciless: Broadcom fell over 12%, dragging Nvidia, AMD, and the entire Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down with it.

The blow was compounded by a blockbuster May jobs report released the same morning. The U.S. economy added 172,000 nonfarm payrolls — more than double the consensus estimate of 80,000. While strong employment figures normally signal a healthy economy, the number reignited fears about the Federal Reserve keeping interest rates elevated for longer. The 10-year Treasury yield surged above 4.5% and the 30-year topped 5%, raising the cost of capital for the debt-heavy AI infrastructure build-out that has powered the Nasdaq's extraordinary 18-month rally.

✓ Analysis — A correction, not a crash

No index breached the 10% threshold that separates a healthy pullback from a true bear-market decline. Every durable bull market in history has been punctuated by corrections of this magnitude — and every one of them, in retrospect, was a buying opportunity for investors who kept their nerve.

Why did the Nasdaq fall harder than the rest?

The severity of Friday's Nasdaq decline is striking, but it tells a story of concentration rather than broad market collapse. Drill into the S&P 500's internals and the picture becomes far less alarming: nearly half of the index's 500 components actually closed higher on Friday. Coca-Cola gained 3%, Colgate surged 4%, and Johnson & Johnson added 2%. Investors were not fleeing the market — they were rotating out of expensive growth names into boring, cash-generating defensive businesses.

The Nasdaq's steeper loss reflects its structural overweight to semiconductors and AI infrastructure. These stocks have been priced to near-perfection, trading at earnings multiples that leave no room for disappointment. When Broadcom blinked, the entire AI trade repriced simultaneously.

"Nearly half of S&P 500 components closed in the green. This was not a market panic — it was a surgical rotation out of tech into defensive quality."

📈 Context — Repricing ahead of mega IPOs

The tech sector's pullback is not happening in a vacuum. Wall Street is gearing up for a wave of landmark public listings. The valuation reset creates breathing room for new capital to enter, and a lower price level for the coming IPO rush.

Think like a general, not a retreating soldier

Military strategy offers the sharpest lens through which to view a market downturn. A great commander does not retreat at the first artillery volley — he repositions, reads the terrain, identifies the enemy's weakness, and strikes decisively. The panicked investor who sells into a correction is the soldier who turns and runs. The disciplined investor who deploys cash into fallen high-quality stocks is the general who turns the battle.

Red days on Wall Street are not days of danger for those who have done their homework. They are discount days on world-class businesses. The cash sitting idle in your brokerage account is your ammunition. The question is not whether to deploy it — it is where, and at precisely what price.

Calculate intrinsic value first

Never buy a falling stock simply because it has fallen. Compute the business's true intrinsic value using discounted cash flows or a conservative earnings multiple, then act only when the price offers a meaningful margin of safety.

Target stocks below 50% of fair value

The 50% threshold is not arbitrary. It is the margin of safety Benjamin Graham identified as sufficient to absorb errors in your analysis while still generating substantial returns when the price recovers.

Your time horizon is your greatest edge

Panic belongs to those measuring performance in days or weeks. The investor with a three-to-five-year horizon experiences corrections not as disasters but as accelerators — they compress the time it takes to reach an attractive entry point.

Diversification is structural protection

Friday's healthcare, consumer staples, and energy sectors held firm. A well-diversified portfolio does not merely survive corrections — it generates dry powder from its defensive holdings that can be redeployed into the discounted growth assets.

What the masters of investing say about days like this

The great investors of the past century built their fortunes not during bull markets — but during the moments of maximum fear that the rest of the market was running from. Their words, written in different eras, ring with uncommon precision today.

"Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful."

Warren Buffett — Chairman, Berkshire Hathaway

"In the short run, the market is a voting machine. In the long run, it is a weighing machine."

Benjamin Graham — Father of Value Investing

"The best time to buy stocks is when there is blood in the streets — even if some of that blood is your own."

John Templeton — Pioneer of Global Investing

"Stocks decline so that the intelligent investor gets a chance to buy them cheaper."

Peter Lynch — Former Manager, Magellan Fund

Friday's selloff is a healthy correction inside a broader bull market — not a structural collapse warranting panic. The tech sector is repricing itself ahead of a new cycle of mega-IPOs and a fresh wave of institutional capital. The investor who reads today's red tape as a clearance sale on great businesses — and who has done the work to know which ones deserve that conviction — is the investor who builds lasting wealth. The market does not punish patience. It rewards it generously, and on a long enough timeline, always has.
