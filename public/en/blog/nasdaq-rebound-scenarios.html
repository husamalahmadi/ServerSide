Three markets in three days

Wall Street lived through three markets in three days. On Wednesday, investors cheered a decisive Democratic midterm sweep and the Dow jumped more than 1,500 points. On Thursday, the same result was re-read as a direct threat to the AI build-out, and the Nasdaq suffered its heaviest one-day fall since April 2025. On Friday, bargain hunters stepped back in. The question now is which of those three days best describes what comes next.

From euphoria to an AI tech wreck

The post-election rally on Wednesday was broad: the Dow rose about 3% while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq gained 1.6% and 1.4%. Chipmakers and data-center operators were already lagging, because Democrats had made gains in key data-center states such as Texas and Georgia. In Texas, governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has called for an immediate moratorium on new data centers.

On Thursday that undercurrent became a flood. The Dow fell more than 1,800 points, all three major averages posted their worst day since April 2025, and the small-cap Russell 2000 also slid into correction. The Nasdaq closed down 5.52%, landing in correction territory, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost more than 12%. The so-called Magnificent Seven alone shed roughly $1 trillion in market value, and Micron (MU) dropped about 17%.

Friday's rebound, and its limits

Friday brought relief. The Nasdaq added 264 points to 24,579.42, the chip index bounced 4.21%, and Nvidia, TSMC, Tesla and Micron all posted solid gains. The bounce was narrow. The S&P 500 rose just 0.31% and the Dow lost 237 points, while the equal-weight S&P 500 ETF (RSP) actually finished lower — a sign that the average stock did not participate.

A hot jobs report

October payrolls came in at 165,000 against a consensus near 65,000, and unemployment slipped to 4.1%. That pushed the odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike in December to about 77% — a headwind for long-duration tech valuations.

Microsoft steps back from Oracle

Bloomberg reported that Microsoft chose not to renew an AI compute lease with Oracle. That feeds the same debate about AI spending that triggered Thursday's slide.

The question that changed

The market is no longer asking whether AI is real. It is asking who will pay for the power, the land and the permits — and at what interest rate.

Why the three indexes split

The divergence is the key to the scenarios. The Nasdaq is a concentrated bet on AI infrastructure, so it took the full force of the policy shock and the full benefit of the bounce. The S&P 500 sits in between: mega-cap tech now carries so much weight that, as Quilter's Stuart Clark noted, weakness in a handful of chip names quickly becomes a sizeable fall for the whole benchmark. The Dow, heavier in healthcare, financials and industrials, is where money rotated on Wednesday — and why it finished the week down less than 1% despite Thursday's 1,800-point drop.

Underneath sits a valuation question that predates the election. BlackRock has flagged hyperscaler capex near $650 billion while incremental margins have slipped to about 28% from 40%. The midterms did not create that doubt. They gave it a catalyst.

Three scenarios for the weeks ahead

The probabilities below are editorial judgement, not a forecast model. Levels are reference points drawn from this week's closes.

Base case · about 50%

A choppy range while the market waits

Friday's bounce holds, but no one rushes to pay up for AI before the new Congress takes office in January and before the Fed's December 9 decision. History supports patience: past Nasdaq corrections show tech tends to find its footing over three to six months, though trading stays rocky in the near term.

  • Nasdaq: oscillates between Thursday's low area, near 24,300, and Wednesday's close near 25,700. Semis remain the swing factor.
  • S&P 500: holds roughly 7,250–7,600. Breadth slowly improves as financials and healthcare do the work.
  • Dow: the relative winner, holding 51,000–53,000 as rotation into older-economy sectors continues.
Bull case · about 25%

Thursday was capitulation

Investors conclude that data-center policy means delays rather than a halt. MAI Capital's Chris Grisanti still expects the build-out deals to get done eventually. Strong AI earnings later this month and steady demand commentary from chipmakers reignite buying.

  • Nasdaq: reclaims 25,000 quickly, then retests the 25,700 post-election close. Leadership returns to semis and memory.
  • S&P 500: recovers toward 7,600–7,690 as mega-caps lift the cap-weighted index.
  • Dow: lags in relative terms as money flows back out of defensives, but still drifts higher.
Bear case · about 25%

A dead-cat bounce

The policy threat turns concrete — state moratoriums, federal scrutiny of AI spending — more capex contracts are trimmed, and a hot economy locks in a December hike that lifts bond yields. Friday's weak breadth would then look like a warning, not a footnote.

  • Nasdaq: breaks Thursday's 24,315 close. A further 5–8% slide would put it around 22,600–23,300 and bring bear-market talk into play.
  • S&P 500: loses the 7,350 area and drifts toward 7,000–7,100.
  • Dow: falls less, but likely slips below 50,000 if rate-hike fears spill into cyclicals.

The bottom line

Friday was a relief rally, not yet a recovery. A 1% gain after a 5.5% drop recovers only about a fifth of the damage, and it came with weak participation outside tech. The single most useful signal over the next two weeks is whether the Nasdaq can hold above Thursday's close while breadth improves. If both happen, the base case leans bullish. If the index slips back below that level on rising yields, the bear case moves to the front.

For long-term investors, a correction driven by policy fears rather than collapsing earnings has historically been an opportunity — one best approached in stages, not all at once.
